4. OCCUPATIONAL THERAPY (OT)
  • Community Independence / Life Skills: Building age-appropriate community independence is a treatment priority. Evidence-based approaches:
    - Community-Based Instruction (CBI): Direct skill practice in real community settings (grocery store, pharmacy, public transit, bank, restaurant). CBI is the evidence-based standard for generalizing community skills in ASD; skills are targeted using task analysis and reinforcement in the natural environment, not simulated practice alone.
    - Functional living skills assessment: Use a standardized tool (e.g., Assessment of Functional Living Skills [AFLS], community domain; TEACCH Transition Assessment Profile) to identify skill targets and measure progress over time. Goals should reflect family priorities and the individual's community participation needs.
    - Graduated independence protocol: Systematically fade adult support from full prompting → partial prompting → supervised independence → independent. Establish mastery criteria before fading; do not require errorless performance prior to increasing independence opportunities.
    - Technology as compensatory strategy: Explicitly teach smartphone navigation apps, contactless payment, accessibility features, and communication apps as tools that extend functional independence (not as shortcuts, but as legitimate accommodations).
    - Transition planning: IEP transition goals should include community access and independent living skills (IDEA mandates transition planning from age 16; best practice per NTACT-C guidelines is age 14). Florida Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) and Agency for Persons with Disabilities (APD) Medicaid waiver programs may fund community-based support services for eligible individuals; discuss eligibility and application timeline with family.
  • Seating and positioning: OT assessment of seating, postural support, and classroom ergonomics. Appropriate positioning is a prerequisite for sustained attention, fine motor output, and sensory regulation; inadequate seating is a modifiable barrier to academic participation.

Non-SSRI agents have stronger ASD-specific evidence:
  • Mirtazapine (preferred first-line if medication needed): start 3.75 mg at bedtime; increase by 3.75 mg weekly; maximum 45 mg/day. Common adverse effects: drowsiness, weight gain, increased appetite.
  • Buspirone (preferred first-line alternative): start 2.5 mg each morning; increase by 2.5 mg weekly to target 15 mg twice daily. Open-label data in ASD: 41–58% positive response. Minimal adverse effect profile. Allow 2–4 weeks for onset.
  • Guanfacine ER (if co-occurring ADHD, sleep disruption, or irritability): start 1 mg; titrate to 4 mg/day maximum. Addresses multiple targets simultaneously.
  • SSRIs (second-line, after above fail): use with caution in ASD; behavioral activation (increased energy, impulsiveness, hyperactivity, insomnia, stereotypy) common; start at lowest available dose; monitor closely for first 2-4 weeks; black box warning for suicidality counseled. If behavioral activation occurs: hold the dose increase, return to the prior tolerated dose, and contact the prescribing provider within 24-48 hours.
  • Benzodiazepines: avoid chronic use; paradoxical activation risk in ASD; short-term bridge use only if needed during acute episodes.
  • IEP/504: anxiety-driven school avoidance, school refusal, and performance anxiety should be addressed in educational plan.
  • Psychiatry co-management for medication management; via existing referral.
