Early behavioral intervention is recommended given their age and the following documented developmental impairments: motor skill impairments affecting daily functioning. ABA-based services are the most extensively studied and supported intervention for young children with ASD or suspected ASD. Recommended intensity to be determined by the treating BCBA following individualized skills assessment; services should span home, clinic, and natural-environment settings, with caregiver coaching embedded throughout and coordination with Early Intervention services.
Families who have access to Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Intervention (NDBI) programs (e.g., Early Start Denver Model, Pivotal Response Training, JASPER) should engage or continue with these approaches. NDBI-based ABA carries a particularly strong evidence base for children in the infant-toddler-preschool period and represents a preferred style of early intervention when accessible.
  • Parent/caregiver training: Caregiver-implemented intervention is a required and evidence-based component of ABA services; specific training content is determined by the treating BCBA and commonly spans the function of target behaviors, reinforcement strategies in natural routines, generalization of clinic and school skills to the home, and crisis response for high-intensity behaviors. Parent training should be documented as a distinct billable service, not subsumed under child direct therapy hours.
  • Least restrictive and assent-based practice: We support programming that uses the least restrictive, most naturalistic procedures appropriate to the child's profile, seeks patient assent as developmentally appropriate, honors and documents assent withdrawal, and relies on positive-reinforcement-based strategies as first-line, with restrictive procedures reserved for documented justification and oversight, consistent with BACB ethical standards.

── Problem 2: Persistent Toe Walking (R26.89) ──

Assessment:
  Persistent toe walking reported. Toe walking is considered idiopathic only when it is bilateral, present from the start of walking, persists beyond age 2 years, and has no neurologic cause; co-occurring autism or ADHD does not exclude it.¹ Toe walking is more common in autistic children and is more likely to persist without treatment than in other children.²,³
  Examination and history: unilateral or asymmetric toe walking or gait; spasticity, brisk reflexes, or clonus; weakness, Gowers sign, calf enlargement, or reduced reflexes. {specify the findings present}
  Features suggesting a neurologic or neuromuscular cause are present. Unilateral or asymmetric toe walking can be the presenting sign of previously undiagnosed cerebral palsy.⁴

Plan:
  • Pediatric neurology: referral for the features above (see Medical Referrals).
  • Neuroimaging: brain MRI to be considered by neurology given upper motor neuron signs.⁵
  • Laboratory: serum creatine kinase recommended given weakness signs and low muscle tone with toe walking. Diagnosis of muscle disease such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy is often delayed, and CK is the screening test.⁵,⁶
