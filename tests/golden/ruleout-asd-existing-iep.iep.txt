I am a pediatrician at the UF Behavior and Development Clinic. I evaluated {Student Name} on {Date of Evaluation} and am writing to provide a formal medical recommendation in support of the family's request for special education services under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA).

They were referred to this clinic for evaluation of concerns in behavioral regulation and sensory processing. A comprehensive diagnostic evaluation was completed at the visit referenced above. Autism Spectrum Disorder was formally considered, and full DSM-5 diagnostic criteria are not met at this evaluation; an autism diagnosis is not being assigned. The evaluation did, however, document an anxiety disorder, which independently affects educational performance. Ruling out autism does not diminish the functional impairments described below, which were directly observed at this evaluation and stand independent of diagnostic labeling. Under IDEA, eligibility turns on whether the district's evaluation identifies one of the disability categories defined at 34 C.F.R. §300.8 and whether, by reason of that disability, the student needs special education and related services. A specific medical diagnosis is neither required for nor sufficient to establish eligibility. In making that determination the team must draw on information from a variety of sources, expressly including information about the student's physical condition and adaptive behavior (34 C.F.R. §300.306(c)(1)(i)), and the evaluation must be sufficiently comprehensive to identify all special education and related services needs, whether or not those needs are commonly linked to the disability category in which the student is classified (34 C.F.R. §300.304(c)(6)).

Speech-language pathology, occupational therapy, and physical therapy are related services available under any IDEA eligibility category (34 C.F.R. §300.34). The sensory findings described in this letter support those services regardless of which category the team determines; they are not themselves eligibility categories, and the absence of an autism diagnosis does not bear on them.

These conditions result in the following challenges that require specialized support in the educational setting:
  • Behavior: Difficulty with emotional regulation and behavioral compliance that interrupts instruction for this student and peers; inflexibility with routine changes and transitions that causes significant distress and limits engagement with non-preferred tasks.
  • Sensory processing: Sensory processing differences that interfere with the ability to sustain attention, remain regulated, and access the curriculum in a typical classroom environment.

RECOMMENDED SERVICES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
I recommend the following updates to the current IEP:

Special Education (Specialized Academic Instruction): Specially designed instruction, delivered by a qualified ESE teacher, is recommended to address academic goals aligned with the identified areas of need. Specialized academic instruction should be individually tailored to the student's present levels of performance and updated at each annual IEP review.

Counseling and Social-Emotional Support Services: School-based counseling is recommended to address the social-emotional and behavioral regulation needs documented above. Goals: anxiety management strategies (graduated exposure, relaxation techniques, cognitive coping) adapted for the school setting; coping skills for flexibility and transition management; social skill generalization in the school environment, reinforcing goals targeted in specialized social skills instruction.

Social Skills Instruction: Structured social skills instruction within the school setting is recommended. Goals: peer interaction initiation, conversational reciprocity, cooperative work skills, and understanding and navigating social expectations in group academic and recess settings. At this developmental stage, goals should also address social communication in vocational and community contexts.

Extended School Year (ESY): ESY services are recommended based on the documented risk of significant skill regression during extended breaks from school. Students with this level of documented need in communication, behavioral regulation, and adaptive skills are at elevated risk for regression during summer break, with disproportionate recovery time upon return to the school year. ESY eligibility and services should be formally determined by the IEP team based on documented regression data; this clinical evaluation supports the team's consideration of ESY services.

Sensory Accommodations: Formal sensory accommodations are recommended as part of the educational plan, developed in collaboration with an OT with sensory processing expertise. Accommodations should be individualized based on OT evaluation (Sensory Profile 2 and/or SPM-2) and may include: noise-canceling headphones, scheduled sensory breaks, a designated calm space, modified lighting, alternative seating (wobble stool, floor seating), and a sensory toolkit accessible to the student throughout the school day.

RECOMMENDED ACCOMMODATIONS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
I recommend that the IEP team consider the following accommodations, calibrated to evaluation findings and individual educational need. These are clinical recommendations, not legal entitlements; the team's selection should reflect what the evaluation supports.

Structure and Predictability Accommodations (implementation calibrated to developmental level and individual presentation):
  • Predictable daily schedule with advance written or visual notice before transitions (schedule format and complexity calibrated to developmental level)

Individually Indicated Accommodations Based on Evaluation Findings:
  • Sensory accommodations: reduced auditory and visual stimulation as needed; scheduled movement breaks; sensory toolkit (noise-canceling headphones, fidget tools, sunglasses); alternative seating options (wiggle cushion, standing desk)

Behavioral and Emotional Accommodations:
  • Anxiety: designated calm-down space, graduated support for anxiety triggers, flexible deadlines for anxiety-driven avoidance, advance notice before schedule changes
  • Emotional regulation: access to a designated calm-down space with sensory tools; break pass for self-initiated regulation breaks (no penalty); de-escalation plan documented in IEP and accessible to all staff; staff trained in recognizing early signs of dysregulation; flexible pacing and reduced pressure during high-dysregulation periods; advance notice of schedule changes
  • Transition supports: individual advance warning before all transitions (verbal cue plus visual timer, minimum 5 minutes prior); visual schedule available at desk and on classroom board showing the full day sequence, with notation of any deviations from routine; first-then board or transition object to support predictability during activity changes; extended transition time built into schedule between high-demand activities; designated consistent staff greeter at morning arrival to establish routine and support regulation at the start of the school day; structured and consistent arrival and dismissal routine (same staff, same location, same sequence each day); early release from class before hallway crowding at dismissal to reduce sensory and transition load; parent-school daily communication system to flag morning home state that may affect school regulation (e.g., brief arrival note, communication app, or teacher check-in at drop-off)
